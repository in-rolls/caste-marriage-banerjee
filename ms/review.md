The same-caste association in letter consideration reproduces. The claim that caste preferences impose little economic cost depends on additional modeling choices and much weaker evidence about actual marriages.

This review concerns Banerjee, Duflo, Ghatak, and Lafortune, *Marry for What? Caste and Mate Selection in Modern India*, AEJ: Microeconomics 5(2), 2013, pp. 33–72. The final article and its author-hosted appendix were read locally. The original 114406 archive is preserved. This is an R reproduction of the main preference regressions and a code and interpretation audit of the matching exercise. It is not an independent new-data replication or a complete rerun of the full-market simulations.

**Who made these decisions?**

The study collected 22,210 matrimonial advertisements in Anandabazar Patrika in 2002–03, then excluded ads giving a telephone number and sampled 784 advertisers; 783 participated. The paper describes a mostly Bengali upper-middle-class market: about 85% have at least a bachelor's degree and about a quarter are Brahmin. It explicitly says the sample is not representative of India or West Bengal. Interviews usually involved the parent, uncle, or older brother managing the search (pp. 46–48). Interpreting the responses as the prospective spouse's individual preferences is consequently too strong.

The archive contains 9,579 letter records, with missing information reducing the principal regression samples to 5,628 letters received by 506 advertisers seeking a groom and 3,944 letters received by 277 advertisers seeking a bride. At follow-up, 750 advertisers were found, 346 reported a marriage or engagement, and spouse characteristics are available for 289. The original program defines “considered” from letters the advertiser planned to pursue. That is shortlisting; it is not a completed marriage or a record of money surrendered. [Sample counts](../output/sample_inventory.csv), [follow-up counts](../output/funnel.csv).

**Endogamy and caste rank are distinct in the specification**

The regressions include an own-caste indicator, the prospective partner's broad caste category, separate terms for the man being higher or lower in rank, and interactions with stated caste preferences. The appendix ranks Brahmin above Baidya, Kshatriya, and Kayastha; these are the authors' classifications. The same-caste definition distinguishes some subcastes within the same broad rank. A Kulin Brahmin and Nath Brahmin can therefore count as different castes with zero rank distance (p. 46). The analysis is not merely comparing “my caste or higher” with everyone below.

The authors describe women's preferences as primarily horizontal and find some tendency for men to marry upward when marrying out of caste (p. 55). The main same-caste coefficients are 13.17 and 17.07 percentage points. The models with a separate same-broad-caste indicator retain coefficients of 13.47 and 17.69 points. These facts support an endogamy association beyond a simple rank ordering. They do not identify a psychological taste separately from beliefs about compatibility, family acceptance, economic security, or other unobserved attributes signaled by caste. Conditioning on reported education and income does not make those alternatives disappear. Nor does an insignificant rank slope establish that rank is irrelevant.

**The principal association survives the checks**

All 28 displayed caste coefficients checked across Tables 3(1), 3(3), 4(1), and 4(3) reproduce to the printed precision. Twenty-seven of their 28 standard errors also do; the remaining difference is below 0.0001. The two conditional-logit same-caste coefficients reproduce, although separation makes some caste-preference interactions poorly determined. The conditional logit is unweighted in the supplied code even though the table notes describe weighted regressions. [Published comparison](../output/published_comparison.csv).

Letters from one advertiser share a decision maker and a shortlist, so the inference checks cluster by advertiser. Clustering changes the main same-caste SEs from 3.29 to 3.04 percentage points for groom searches and from 3.51 to 3.71 for bride searches. Both associations remain clear. Across all 506 and 277 advertiser deletions, the estimates remain positive and significant; the ranges are 12.40–13.98 and 15.73–18.86 percentage points. Wild-bootstrap p-values from 9,999 draws are at or below 0.0001. A reported zero is simulation resolution, not a literal zero probability.

Unweighted estimation, equal total weight per advertiser, restricting to known caste, and trimming weights at their 99th percentile preserve the main same-caste association. Bonferroni adjustment across 14 selected caste coefficients in the two baseline models also preserves it. This family is explicitly defined; it is not a correction for every coefficient in the article. Advertisers were sampled, but caste was not randomly assigned, so no artificial caste-label randomization test is supplied. [Sensitivity results](../output/robustness.csv), [multiplicity](../output/multiplicity.csv), [deletions](../output/leave_one_advertiser_out.csv).

One apparent coding defect was a rejected substantive criticism. A same-caste rule uses an impossible conjunction where a disjunction appears intended. Correcting it changes zero pairs in either main regression sample. The typo cannot explain these results. [Affected-pair check](../output/caste_typo_impact.csv).

**What would they give up?**

The simplified groom-income specification reproduces a same-caste coefficient of 0.13954 and a predicted-log-income coefficient of 0.34780. Their ratio is 0.4012 log points: exp(0.4012) − 1 implies a 49.4% outside-caste predicted-income premium. Equivalently, the same-caste option can have 33.1% less predicted income. Across 1,999 advertiser bootstrap samples, re-estimating both the income prediction and consideration regression, the 95% percentile intervals are 24.6–82.1% for the premium and 19.8–45.1% for the discount. The income coefficient is positive in all these draws. [Complete-procedure bootstrap](../output/income_bootstrap_summary.csv).

That coefficient ratio isolates the same-caste term in the reference category. It is not a universal price of avoiding lower castes. The full contrast includes the prospective groom's caste category, rank difference, and the advertiser's stated preference. For a Brahmin family with no stated caste preference, a Baidya groom requires an estimated 6.3% higher predicted income for equal consideration, with a 95% bootstrap interval from −22.4% to 49.9%. A Kayastha groom requires 63.4%, with an interval from 30.9% to 107.8%. A negative endpoint permits the alternative to be preferred without an income premium. These comparisons hold other included characteristics fixed and use the same bootstrap draws. [Full caste comparisons](../output/lower_caste_income_contrasts.csv).

These are conversions of a linear shortlisting regression, not cash payments, realized earnings sacrifices, or experimentally identified willingness to pay. Predicted income is built from education and occupation; its coefficient can reflect attributes associated with those predictors rather than the effect of offering extra cash. The estimates concern families' recorded consideration of the letters they received, with sample weights correcting the deliberate sampling of considered and unconsidered letters. They do not reveal choices over every possible spouse.

**The structural conclusions need a separate audit**

The matching simulation is not simply the headline weighted regressions carried forward. The supplied bootstrap code omits their weights. Its mean same-caste coefficients are 0.1570 and 0.2022, close to the unweighted regressions' 0.1589 and 0.1986, rather than the weighted 0.1317 and 0.1707. The archive supplies 1,000 coefficient draws; the main Matlab loop uses the first 250. This changes the estimated preferences entering the equilibrium exercise. [Coefficient handoff](../output/bootstrap_handoff.csv).

There are two concrete implementation problems. First, the male preference calculation's same-residence feature compares the man's missing-residence flag with the woman's residence category, while also checking his family-origin field as though it were a missing-residence flag. Correcting the column references changes 1,678,947 of the 113,914,536 possible candidate pairs, about 1.47%. That is a feature error; it does not establish how many equilibrium matches would change. Second, expressions such as `coefficient * education == 2` compare the product to 2 instead of multiplying by the education-category indicator. The supplied quality calculations consequently omit their intended education contributions for 6,085 men and 12,414 women. This affects the quality index used in evaluation; it is not evidence that all matching preferences lose their education terms. [Residence check](../output/residence_feature_error.csv), [quality check](../output/quality_education_error.csv).

The native workflow also has incomplete handoffs. The correlation scripts require `data/id_mailbox`, which is absent from the package and not constructed in the supplied main preparation script. The main script saves `data/stable` before constructing some variables later required by the matching summaries. These are obstacles to running the archive exactly as documented. They require reconstruction, not silently invented files.

**Does the model fit marriages and bound their costs?**

Published Table 6 predicts 93% same-caste marriages without search frictions, with a 2.5–97.5 percentile range of 83–99%; the observed figure is 69%, with a range of 64–75%. Predicted height correlation is 0.86 versus 0.39 observed, and family-origin correlation is 1.00 versus 0.51. These are material discrepancies in matching patterns. The paper acknowledges overprediction of endogamy and rejection of a joint fit test; the criticism is how much confidence to place in counterfactual costs given that acknowledged fit, not an allegation that the authors conceal it.

Counting the displayed no-friction Table 6 values gives 12 of 21 observed point estimates inside the simulation intervals and 14 overlapping intervals. This is a check of rounded published numbers, not an independent simulation. The text reports 14 and 15. The substantive discrepancies above are more consequential than this counting disagreement. [Transcribed fit check](../output/published_matching_fit.csv).

Table 8's “marrying within caste” and “marrying a higher caste” columns both use marrying a lower-caste partner as the reference. The small point estimates do not establish negligible opportunity costs. For men, the within-caste education estimate is 0.0011 categories, with a percentile interval from −1.8724 to 1.8661; the income estimate is ₹1,755.83, with an interval from −₹75,943.67 to ₹54,369.01. Those ranges allow economically substantial differences. A credible negligible-cost claim needs an explicitly small meaningful bound, with uncertainty narrow enough to exclude larger costs. Failure to reject zero is insufficient.

Nor does a model of this selected marriage market establish that economic growth will fail to weaken caste or that caste has little aggregate economic cost. Those claims involve changing preferences, other marriage channels, labor markets, and other populations outside the demonstrated comparison.

**Coverage and the twelve review moves**

| Review move | Finding or limit |
|---|---|
| Index decomposition | Education terms disappear from the supplied quality calculation through an operator-precedence error; affected people are counted. |
| Baselines and gaps | Separate same-caste shortlisting advantages from caste-specific comparisons and the observed/simulated endogamy gap. |
| Dose response | There is no assigned treatment dose; rank differences are observational and their equal numerical spacing is an assumption. |
| Mechanism | Own-caste association is measured; taste, beliefs, family acceptance, and other caste signals are not separately identified. |
| Implementation | Weighting changes at the simulation handoff; a residence feature uses the wrong columns; native summary handoffs are incomplete. |
| ITT to TOT | Inapplicable: this is not an encouragement experiment with an identified compliance rate. |
| Magnitude benchmarks | Reconstruct within-paper income and rank contrasts. No new web search was conducted after the user prohibited it. These conversions are not external causal benchmarks. |
| Statistical fragility | Advertiser clustering, wild bootstrap, all advertiser deletions, weight changes, and a defined multiplicity family preserve the principal association. |
| Stated versus revealed | Planned pursuit of letters, usually reported by relatives, is distinct from completed marriage and cash sacrifice. |
| Companion evidence | The supplied appendix qualifies the fit and shows alternative matching assumptions; no additional outside-paper search is claimed. |
| Selective emphasis | Robust own-caste coefficients coexist with uncertain rank contrasts, substantial model misfit, and broad opportunity-cost intervals. |
| Generalizability | One selected Bengali newspaper marriage market, not India, West Bengal as a whole, or all economic consequences of caste. |

The reproduced calculations cover six main linear regressions and two conditional-logit fits, the simplified groom-income point estimates, bootstrap uncertainty for income contrasts, and the stated robustness and code checks. The simplified bride-income column currently differs from the publication and produces rank-deficient income predictions; no bride-income WTP is reported as reproduced. The full 250-draw Matlab matching exercise, Bayesian Figure 1, and every auxiliary appendix regression have not been rerun. This repository therefore supports the main same-caste association and identifies specific limitations of the structural claims; it does not claim to reproduce all counterfactual outcomes or quantify how the code repairs change equilibrium welfare.
